Passages

Discover literature through the writing itself, one passage at a time, and begin reading whenever something catches you.

Why this exists

Think of the last book you read because you wanted to, not because you had to. Chances are you didn't find it in a ranked list. Someone read you a paragraph, or a line turned up somewhere and stayed with you, and you went looking for the rest.

Thousands of classic works are free to read, and many people never begin them. The obstacle usually isn't price. It's everything that comes before the first page. Books reach us through covers, reviews and bestseller lists, rarely through the writing itself. Some arrive with a reputation for difficulty, and start to feel like an obligation instead of an invitation. It's hard to commit to 600 pages without having read one. And most of us only ever meet a narrow range of authors, languages and traditions.

Passages lets you meet the writing first. Each screen holds a passage chosen to show what a work is really like. If one catches you, the whole work is right there, ready to read. The aim isn't to tell you what you ought to read. It's to help you find something you want to read.

How it works

  • The feed. One passage per screen; the text comes first, with title and author kept small. Swipe up for the next.
  • Tap to learn more. You get a spoiler-free description of the work, a note on why that passage was picked, and an honest sense of the commitment: roughly how long it takes to read, and whether it starts slowly.
  • Read the whole thing. Every work opens at its first page in a simple reader. It remembers where you stopped, and when you reach the passage that brought you there, it's highlighted.
  • Already know the book? You can jump straight to the passage instead.
  • Save and share. Keep the passages you like, or send one to someone as a link. The works you've started wait for you under the bookmark at the top, so you can pick up where you left off.
  • A feed that adapts to you. It notices which passages you open, save and start reading and shows you more of what you respond to. About a third of what it shows stays random, so you keep meeting the unfamiliar. If a passage isn't for you, say so and you won't see it again. You can also hide whole forms, poetry for instance, or switch the learning off, in Settings.

What it stands for

Access comes first. Public-domain works stay free to discover and read. The core of Passages will never depend on a subscription or on what you can pay.

Reading matters more than scrolling. The feed is a way into literature, not an end in itself. There are no streaks and no nagging notifications. If you leave after two minutes and spend the evening reading a book, it has done its job.

Literature should be broad. The collection makes room for different languages, cultures, periods and forms, including writers who've had less attention, not only what's already popular.

Editorial judgment remains human. Technology helps find and evaluate candidate passages, but people decide what belongs here and how it's presented.

Your privacy is respected. Passages collects only what it needs to improve the experience, anonymously, and never sells reader data.

The mission guides growth. New features, partnerships and funding are only worth it if they help more people find their way into literature.

How a passage gets here

  1. Only the early part of a work is eligible. For a novel, that's roughly the first 40%, so a passage can't give away how things turn out.
  2. An AI model reads that part and proposes a shortlist. It looks for voice, imagery, humour, insight, feeling and suspense, deliberately avoiding lines that are merely quotable. It also drafts the notes and labels each passage's mood and themes.
  3. A script checks every candidate against the source, word for word, so nothing is misquoted or invented.
  4. A second, independent AI pass scores each one. Would a stranger stop on it? Does it make sense with no context? Does anything give the story away? Weak candidates are cut.
  5. A person reads what's left, edits the notes and decides. The question is simple: would this make me want to know what book it's from?

The AI is there because no one person can read everything worth reading. It doesn't decide what's good. After a passage goes live, readers have the final say: passages that rarely make anyone curious are replaced.

What's in the library

Everything here is in the public domain in the United States, including the translations, so every work can be read freely and in full. Portuguese passages come with an optional English translation, made by AI and labelled as such.

Privacy

There are no accounts. Your saves, settings, reading positions and the feed's sense of your taste are stored only in your browser, on your device. Clearing your browser data erases them. None of it is sent anywhere.

To find out whether the idea works, the site counts anonymous events such as "a passage was opened" or "a book was started," using GoatCounter. It uses no cookies and records nothing that identifies you.

Where things stand

Passages is an independent project and an early prototype. Right now it's trying to answer a few questions. Can a short passage make someone genuinely want to read a whole work? Does removing the friction between discovering a work and starting it help people read? Can a feed send people out of the feed and into the writing? And can it introduce readers to works they'd never otherwise meet?

Public-domain works are where it starts, because they can be offered complete and free. The ambition reaches further. Possible directions include:

  • accounts, so your reading follows you between devices
  • ways to read with others: collections, notes in the margins, passages passed between friends
  • contemporary works, through publishers and libraries, on terms that respect writers
  • a place for living writers to reach readers
  • many more languages and traditions

None of these exists yet, and each will have to serve the mission. In the meantime, expect things to change, and occasionally break.
